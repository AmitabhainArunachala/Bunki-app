## Key question 1 — How JPDB, Migaku, LingQ, Satori Reader and Anki/FSRS represent per-item knowledge and compute text difficulty/coverage

### Takeaway
Every shipping system reduces per-item knowledge to either (a) a small discrete state set derived from scheduler interval (jpdb, Migaku, MorphMan/AnkiMorphs, WaniKani) or (b) a continuous memory state without uncertainty (FSRS: stability S and difficulty D, retrievability R computed on demand). Text difficulty/coverage is then a simple ratio of known tokens or known unique words in the text (LingQ "% new words", Migaku "comprehension score", jpdb "coverage"), optionally with a corpus-level readability formula (Lee & Hasebe) that ignores the learner. None of them stores confidence, evidence tier, or receptive/productive split per item; none documents an interference or confusion model.

**WaniKani (per-item stage model, useful comparator)**
- Nine stages with fixed intervals: Apprentice 1–4 = 4h, 8h, ~1d (23h), ~2d (47h); Guru 1–2 = ~1w, ~2w; Master ~1 month; Enlightened ~4 months; Burned = no more reviews. Failure rule: `incorrect_adjustment_count = ceil(number_of_incorrect_answers / 2)`, `penalty_factor = starting_stage >= 5 ? 2 : 1`, `ending_stage = max(1, starting_stage - incorrect_adjustment_count * penalty_factor)`; both meaning and reading are answered per item (kana-only vocab tests meaning only) (fetched secondary compilation citing WaniKani's knowledge guide) — [Kakehashi research note](https://github.com/Portego-00/Kakehashi/blob/main/research/wanikani-custom-srs-web.md); same formula in [KaniManabu SRScheduler.m](https://github.com/Atelier-Shiori/KaniManabu) and [hakubun](https://github.com/salemlf/hakubun)

**Anki / FSRS**
- Memory state per card is two numbers, Stability (S) and Difficulty (D ∈ [1,10]); Retrievability R is computed, not stored. "No explicit uncertainty or confidence metric is stored" (fetched) — [awesome-fsrs wiki: The Algorithm](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm)
- FSRS-6: 21 parameters, defaults `[0.212, 1.2931, 2.3065, 8.2956, 6.4133, 0.8334, 3.0194, 0.001, 1.8722, 0.1666, 0.796, 1.4835, 0.0614, 0.2629, 1.6483, 0.6014, 1.8729, 0.5425, 0.0912, 0.0658, 0.1542]`; forgetting curve `R(t,S) = (1 + factor · t/S)^(−w20)` with factor chosen so R(S,S)=0.9; initial difficulty `D0(G) = w4 − e^{w5(G−1)} + 1`; difficulty update `D' = D + ΔD · (10−D)/9` with mean reversion toward a target; same-day stability `S' = S · e^{w17(G−3+w18)} · S^{−w19}` (FSRS-6); post-lapse stability (v4 form) `S'_f = w11 · D^{−w12} · ((S+1)^{w13} − 1) · e^{w14(1−R)}`; overdue reviews raise the next stability but "converge to an upper limit rather than growing indefinitely" (fetched) — [awesome-fsrs wiki: The Algorithm](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm)
- Stability after successful recall (snippet, FSRS-4.5/5 form): `S' = S · (1 + e^{w8} · (11 − D) · S^{−w9} · (e^{(1−R)·w10} − 1) · hard_penalty · easy_bonus)` — [Expertium: technical explanation of FSRS](https://expertium.github.io/Algorithm.html); [rs-fsrs overview](https://deepwiki.com/open-spaced-repetition/rs-fsrs/3.1-fsrs-algorithm-overview)
- Definitions: "Stability (S): the time, in days, required for R to decrease from 100% to 90%"; default parameters were fit on "several hundred million reviews from ~10k users"; users without enough history should use defaults; desired retention "values between 70% and 97% are considered reasonable" (fetched) — [awesome-fsrs wiki: ABC of FSRS](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/ABC-of-FSRS)
- Benchmark on 10k Anki users / ~350M reviews (no same-day) — log loss / RMSE(bins) / AUC / #params: FSRS-6 0.3460 / 0.0653 / 0.7034 / 21; FSRS-5 0.3561 / 0.0742 / 0.7010 / 19; HLR 0.4694 / 0.1275 / 0.6369 / 3; DASH 0.3682 / 0.0838 / 0.6311 / 9; ACT-R 0.4033 / 0.1074 / 0.5225 / 5; Ebisu v2 0.4989 / 0.1627 / 0.6051 / 0; GRU 0.3328 / 0.0549 / 0.7324 / 503; RWKV-Instant 0.2773 / 0.0250 / 0.8329 / 2.76M; AVG baseline 0.3945 / 0.1034 / 0.4997 (fetched) — [srs-benchmark README](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark/blob/main/README.md)

**MorphMan / AnkiMorphs (Anki-side known-word models used by the Japanese community)**

### Inferences
- The industry norm is **interval-thresholded discrete states** (Migaku ≥21 days ⇒ known; MorphMan mature = 21 days; WaniKani stage ≥ Guru is what most tools treat as "known"). This is a lossy projection of the scheduler state; a design that stores the continuous state (FSRS S, D or a Beta posterior) and derives the discrete label at query time subsumes all of them and can reproduce each tool's label by choosing a threshold.
- Every coverage metric is `known_tokens / tokens` or `known_types / types` with binary "known"; a probabilistic version, `E[coverage] = Σ_tokens P(known_i) / N` with a variance from the Bernoulli terms, is a strict generalization and lets the frontier engine reason about uncertain words rather than excluding them.
- jpdb's "i+1 sentence" claim implies it maintains, per learner, a known-set over (vid,sid) and filters sentence candidates where every other token's (vid,sid) is in that set; the 130M-sentence bank suggests a precomputed per-sentence token-id list so filtering is a set-difference per sentence. The tie-breaking rules are undocumented.
- Satori Reader's furigana rule is the clearest example of a **per-kanji** (not per-word) knowledge projection driving rendering; the same per-kanji known-set can drive reading-drill selection.

- Could not fetch jpdb FAQ/changelog, Migaku docs, LingQ forum, or Satori Reader pages directly; the state semantics for jpdb come from a third-party client's type definitions, not jpdb documentation.
- No documented formula for jpdb's difficulty model, its coverage calculation (tokens vs types, whether "learning" counts), or Migaku's comprehension score.
- Whether Migaku/jpdb track receptive vs productive separately: nothing found; both appear to have one status per word.

## Key question 2 — Knowledge-graph / prerequisite-graph knowledge tracing in research and feasibility for one learner with sparse, noisy evidence

### Takeaway
Research KT (DKT → SAKT/SAINT → GKT → LLM-augmented KT with prerequisite graphs and uncertainty-aware embeddings) is trained on populations of thousands of students and hundreds of thousands of interactions; the models that actually win the largest per-learner memory benchmark are small, interpretable per-item models (FSRS, 21 params) or tiny RNNs (GRU, 503 params), while HLR (3 params) and Ebisu (0 params) are weak. For a device-owned single learner with 10^2–10^4 events, the feasible design is per-item memory states with population priors plus a light graph layer (propagation of evidence to neighbours through explicit typed edges) rather than a neural KT model; the research contributions worth borrowing are (i) prerequisite-graph propagation, (ii) explicit posterior uncertainty (Gaussian/Beta) as the cold-start remedy, (iii) "ghost counts" priors, and (iv) per-lexeme morphological tags as features.

### Cited Findings
- KT model catalogue (implemented in pyKT): DKT, DKVMN, SAKT ("A self-attentive model for knowledge tracing"), SAINT ("Towards an appropriate query, key, and value computation for knowledge tracing"), AKT, GKT, LPKT, simpleKT ("tough-to-beat" baseline), plus ATKT, KQN, HawkesKT, IEKT, SKVMN, QIKT, RKT, DIMKT, ATDKT, SparseKT, FoLiBiKT, DTransformer, stableKT, extraKT, csKT, LefoKT, UKT, HCGKT, MTKT, RobustKT, MoCKT, DenoiseKT — 32 models, 7+ datasets (fetched) — [pyKT toolkit](https://github.com/pykt-team/pykt-toolkit)
- BKT has four/five parameters: prior, learns, forgets, guesses, slips; pyBKT supports per-item guess/slip (KT-IDEM style), per-item learn rates, forgetting, and multi-learn classes; fit by EM; input encoded 0 = no response, 1 = incorrect, 2 = correct (fetched) — [pyBKT](https://github.com/CAHLR/pyBKT)
- GKT ("Graph-based Knowledge Tracing: Modeling Student Proficiency Using Graph Neural Network") propagates a student's hidden state over a concept graph; reference implementation supports a `Dense` graph type and compares to DKT on ASSISTments 2009 skill-builder (fetched; the README does not give the aggregate/update equations) — [jhljx/GKT](https://github.com/jhljx/GKT)
- (snippet) "SAKT was the first model to use the self-attention mechanism in knowledge tracing ... especially good at dealing with situations with small amounts of data"; "SAINT ... employs an encoder-decoder structure"; in a 2024 comparison on an English-learning dataset SAINT had the best AUC 77.14 / ACC 70.13, with SAKT at 847.36k parameters vs SAINT at 71,184.90k — [Bridging the Vocabulary Gap: Using Side Information for Deep Knowledge Tracing (Applied Sciences 2024)](https://doi.org/10.3390/app14198927)
- (snippet) GLLM-KT (2025/26): "a graph-incorporated ultra-small LLM-based knowledge tracing model ... integrates a knowledge prerequisite graph to capture concept dependencies, a structured instruction template ... and an adaptive confidence head to improve prediction robustness"; evaluated on four benchmark datasets — [GLLM-KT (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-92-1468-6_30)
- (snippet) Adaptive G-UKT (2026): "unified probabilistic framework for knowledge tracing via adaptive graph topology learning and uncertainty-aware Gaussian embeddings"; "observation streams are frequently sparse with entities only interacting with a small subset of nodes, and without propagating state information across topologically adjacent nodes, the model cannot infer latent states for unobserved entities"; "explicit posterior uncertainty quantification via diagonal Gaussian covariance is a more principled strategy for mitigating the cold-start problem under sparse observation regimes than implicit adversarial perturbation training" — [Adaptive G-UKT (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13324340/)

- (snippet) Variational Deep Knowledge Tracing for Language Learning (LAK 2021) applied variational latent states to Duolingo data — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3448139.3448170)

- Benchmark evidence on data efficiency: on 10k users, per-item models with 5–21 parameters (DASH, FSRS) beat HLR and Ebisu by a wide margin; a 503-parameter GRU is only slightly better than FSRS-6 (log loss 0.3328 vs 0.3460), while a 2.76M-parameter RWKV is the only model with a large gain (0.2773) (fetched) — [srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark/blob/main/README.md)
- Small hobby precedent: an Android app "Aiko JLPT" declares a knowledge graph with edges "Kanji → Vocabulary", "Vocabulary → Reading/Listening", "Grammar → Reading/Listening", "Vocabulary ↔ Grammar", "JLPT questions → Knowledge items" but documents no learner-state formulas (fetched) — [Aiko-jptl](https://github.com/Linkesh-K-V/Aiko-jptl)

### Inferences
- Feasibility bands for one learner (inference from the benchmark parameter counts and the cold-start literature): with ≤1,000 events, only models with population priors are defensible (FSRS default weights; Beta/Elo priors with pseudo-counts); with 10^3–10^4 events per-learner FSRS-style parameter optimisation becomes possible for the aggregate but not per component; deep KT (SAKT/SAINT/GKT) remains out of reach because it needs many learners to learn item embeddings. A device-local design should therefore be: population-prior per-item model + explicit graph propagation with hand-set (later tuned) edge weights.
- The GKT/G-UKT idea that matters for Japanese is *propagation to unobserved nodes*: a correct reading of 歩道 (ほどう) is weak positive evidence for the 音読み ホ of 歩 and for 道 = ドウ, and, through them, for other words using those readings. This is the mechanism by which a few hundred events can place a bound on tens of thousands of nodes.
- The BKT structure (learn/forget/guess/slip) maps neatly onto evidence tiers: guess/slip are per *observation method*, not per item — a multiple-choice recognition has high guess; a typed reading has near-zero guess but nonzero slip (typos); an LLM-judged free production has both a high guess and a high slip. pyBKT's "multi-guess/slip classes" is exactly the machinery to encode evidence quality.
- HLR's lexeme-tag features are a precedent for tagging each event with the *form* observed (e.g., `食べる<v><past><neg>`), so that evidence on a conjugated form can be split between the lexeme node and the grammar-form node.

### Gaps
- Could not read any KT paper in full (arXiv, Springer, ACM, PMC blocked); the GKT aggregate/update/predict equations and GLLM-KT's confidence-head definition are not verified.
- No paper found that applies GKT/SAKT/BKT specifically to Japanese vocabulary, kanji or readings; the only vocabulary-KT precedents found are Duolingo HLR (lexeme-level), Variational DKT on Duolingo, and "side-information" DKT on an English dataset.
- No published per-learner (n=1) KT evaluation found; feasibility claims above are inference.

## Key question 3 — Representing receptive vs productive knowledge and evidence-quality tiers (graded review vs tutor observation vs exposure): formal models

### Takeaway
The learning literature is consistent that receptive and productive knowledge dissociate (receptive grows faster; productive tasks transfer better) so they must be separate nodes/contracts, not one score. For evidence quality there are two fetched, formal, implementable mechanisms: (1) Ebisu's Bayesian Beta-over-recall model, whose "fuzzy quiz" observation model with parameters q₁ = P(observed success | truly recalled) and q₀ = P(observed success | truly not recalled) is exactly an evidence-quality tier encoded as a noise channel, with moment-matched posteriors; and (2) Pelánek-style Elo with an uncertainty function K(n) = α/(1+βn) per learner and per item, which gives an explicit confidence proxy (attempt count) and per-observation-type guess adjustments. WaniKani is the shipping precedent for separate reading and meaning contracts per item. Bunki's own codex already specifies an A–D evidence hierarchy that these models can parameterise.

### Cited Findings
- (snippet) "Receptive vocabulary develops 1.5 times faster than productive vocabulary because it places less demand on syntax, spelling, pronunciation, and retrieval"; "a systematic recognition-production gap across lexical categories: receptive accuracy consistently exceeded productive accuracy" — [Mobile-assisted academic vocabulary study (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10981021)
- (snippet) "Productive tasks contribute to significantly higher scores than receptive tasks regardless of repetition frequency" (Teng & Xu 2025) — [Pushing vocabulary knowledge from receptive to productive mastery](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/13621688221077028)
- (snippet) Zhai 2026 recommends "contracting spacing for short-term retention of receptive word knowledge and transitioning to expanded spacing for long-term retention of productive word knowledge" — [Optimal Spacing for Types of Word Knowledge](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688261449449)
- (snippet) "targeted task designs — including repetition with variation, spaced/interleaved retrieval practice, and receptive-productive integration tasks — are effective in reducing receptive-productive gaps" — [Repetition, Retrieval, and Spaced Practice](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398256768_Repetition_Retrieval_and_Spaced_Practice)
- WaniKani requires a meaning answer and a reading answer per kanji/vocabulary item (kana-only vocab tests meaning only) and the stage penalty is computed from the number of incorrect answers across both (fetched secondary) — [Kakehashi research note](https://github.com/Portego-00/Kakehashi/blob/main/research/wanikani-custom-srs-web.md)
- Ebisu model: per-item triple `(alpha, beta, t)` meaning "the prior on recall probability after `t` time units since this fact's last review is `Beta(alpha, beta)`"; predicted recall at elapsed time `tnow`, with `δ = tnow/t`: `E[p^δ] = B(α+δ, β)/B(α,β) = Γ(α+β)/Γ(α) · Γ(α+δ)/Γ(α+β+δ)`; posterior after a quiz with k successes of n trials is obtained by weighting with the binomial likelihood, computing the first two moments m₁, m₂ analytically, and moment-matching back to a Beta: `α̂ = (μ(1−μ)/σ² − 1)·μ`, `β̂ = (μ(1−μ)/σ² − 1)·(1−μ)`; "A higher alpha = beta value indicates greater confidence in the half-life estimate (less sensitivity to quiz results), while lower values like alpha = beta = 3 enable aggressive updates"; **fuzzy/noisy quizzes**: "a 'real' binary outcome gets scrambled, with parameters q₁ (probability observed success given real success) and q₀ (probability observed success given real failure)" (fetched) — [fasiha/ebisu](https://github.com/fasiha/ebisu)
- Elo as used by Pelánek's group (fetched code): prediction `sigmoid_shift(G[student] − D[item], random_factor(qtype))` where `random_factor` handles multiple-choice guessing by question type; update `G[s] += ufun_student(attempts_s) · (correct − prediction)`, `D[i] −= ufun_problem(attempts_i) · (correct − prediction)`; uncertainty function `alpha / (1 + beta * x)` with defaults `alpha = 1.0, beta = 0.05`, separate α/β allowed for students vs items; counters per student and per item — [adaptive-learning/geography-analysis models_prior_knowledge.py](https://github.com/adaptive-learning/geography-analysis/blob/master/models_prior_knowledge.py)
- (snippet) Pelánek: "Typical applications include learning of factual knowledge, foreign language vocabulary, or practice of basic skills"; Elo "effectively updates estimates of item difficulties and learner abilities as they evolve" — [Applications of the Elo rating system in adaptive educational systems](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf); (snippet) an Elo-based large-scale system's psychometrics paper (2025) stresses that "reliable ability ratings are the cornerstone of effective adaptation" — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666920X25000165); multi-concept multivariate Elo adapted to medical training data — [arXiv 2403.07908](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.07908)
- HLR encodes historical `history_seen`, `history_correct`, `session_seen`, `session_correct`, `delta` seconds, and (snippet) uses response time "as a stand-in for confidence" (fetched README; snippet for RT) — [halflife-regression](https://github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression)
- Bunki codex (local, fetched): evidence hierarchy Tier A "Direct constrained retrieval with a known answer; result, latency, hints — Strong evidence; may update FSRS"; Tier B "Constrained production with explicit target and versioned rubric — Moderate evidence; can update learner state, conservatively"; Tier C "Free conversation or writing judged by an LLM — Candidate evidence only until calibrated or corroborated" and "Lookup behavior, dwell, reopening, self-reported uncertainty — Diagnostic evidence; never mastery by itself"; Tier D "Passive appearance in a passage — Exposure only"; scheduling "per RetrievalContract, not per broad label"; ten capability labels including "Reading retrieval", "Productive lexical retrieval", "Discrimination: separation from a named confusable" — [JAPANESE_LEARNING_OS_CODEX_V1_FREEZE_2026-07-27.md §5.2–5.4](/home/user/Bunki-app/docs/convergence/JAPANESE_LEARNING_OS_CODEX_V1_FREEZE_2026-07-27.md)

### Inferences
- **Evidence tiers as a noise channel (concrete).** Adopt Ebisu's (q₀, q₁) per observation method: Tier A typed reading: q₁≈0.97 (typo slip), q₀≈0.02; Tier A 4-way multiple choice: q₁≈0.97, q₀≈0.25 (guess); Tier B rubric-scored production: q₁≈0.9, q₀≈0.1; Tier C LLM-judged free output: q₁≈0.8, q₀≈0.3 until calibrated; Tier C lookup (a *failure*-type signal): treat "did not look up" as q₁≈0.6/q₀≈0.5 (nearly uninformative) and "looked up" as a strong negative for the *sense in context* node; Tier D exposure: no likelihood update, only a counter and a small stability bonus if a subsequent probe succeeds. These numbers are placeholders to be calibrated against Tier A probes (the codex's step 6 "compare predictions with subsequent direct probes").
- **Receptive vs productive as separate contracts with a directed prior edge.** Nodes: `Lexeme.recognition`, `Lexeme.reading_retrieval`, `Lexeme.meaning_recall`, `Lexeme.productive_retrieval`. Evidence on productive success implies receptive success (edge weight ≈1.0); receptive success implies productive only weakly (≈0.3–0.5, consistent with the 1.5× growth gap). Failure propagates the other way: productive failure says little about recognition; recognition failure predicts productive failure strongly.
- **Elo vs Beta.** Elo gives a shared scale between learner and items (useful for cold-start placement from frequency rank) but no forgetting curve; Beta/Ebisu gives forgetting with uncertainty but no item-difficulty sharing; FSRS gives the best-calibrated forgetting but no uncertainty. A pragmatic hybrid for n=1: FSRS-6 (default weights) for Tier-A scheduled contracts, and a Beta(α,β) "belief" per node updated by all tiers via (q₀,q₁), with α+β acting as the confidence count that the frontier computation uses.

## Key question 4 — "i+1" sentence selection from a large bank: algorithms for exactly one unknown target, coverage, diversity, pain-node density; how jpdb and community tools do it

### Takeaway
The only fully documented open algorithm is AnkiMorphs' (successor to MorphMan): score = 10⁶ × |unknown morphs| + min(priority terms + quadratic length-deviation terms, 10⁶−1), so every 1-unknown ("1T") card precedes every 2-unknown card regardless of other terms; priority terms use frequency-list rank of unknown/learning/all morphs; "fresh" (recently learned) morphs are reinforced; and cards whose unknown morphs were already seen today are skipped. jpdb advertises automatic i+1 sentence cards from ~130M sentences but publishes no selection rule. Nothing found handles diversity, grammar-form unknowns, or "pain-node density" explicitly; those must be designed.

### Cited Findings
- AnkiMorphs score (fetched docs): `score = min(PU × |M_U| + min(W_P · P + W_D · D, PU − 1), score_max)` with `PU = 10^6`, `score_max = 2^31 − 1 − 10^8`; "incomprehensibility = PU × |M_U|"; "insignificance = W_P · P" over five metrics (total priority of all / unknown / learning morphs; average priority of all / learning morphs); "deviation = W_D · D" using piecewise quadratics around a target morph-count range: `D_target = ceil(a(|n − T|²) + b|n − T| + c)` outside bounds, zero inside; "All 1T cards should appear before any MT cards, regardless of their insignificance or deviation" — [AnkiMorphs recalc docs](https://github.com/mortii/anki-morphs/blob/main/docs/src/user_guide/usage/recalc.md)
- Same in code: `unknown_morphs_amount_score = len(unknown_morphs) * MORPH_UNKNOWN_PENALTY` (`MORPH_UNKNOWN_PENALTY = 1_000_000`); `tuning = all_morphs_total_priority_score + unknown_morphs_total_priority_score + learning_morphs_total_priority_score + all_morphs_avg_priority_score + learning_morphs_avg_priority_score + learning_morphs_target_difference_score + all_morphs_target_difference_score`; `_score = unknown_morphs_amount_score + min(tuning, MORPH_UNKNOWN_PENALTY − 1)`; `due = score = min(_score, _MAX_SCORE)`; cards can be moved to the end of the queue (fetched) — [card_score.py](https://github.com/mortii/anki-morphs/blob/main/ankimorphs/recalc/card_score.py)
- Priority of a morph absent from the frequency list: `no_morph_priority_value = len(morph_priority) + 1` (fetched) — [AnkiMorphs discussion #191](https://github.com/mortii/anki-morphs/discussions/191)
- Design rationale from the same discussion: quadratic (not linear) penalties "on either side of a target length range with no penalty zone in between", asymmetric so "undershooting the target receives less penalty than overshooting"; cards containing *learning* morphs get preferential treatment "which encourages reinforcement of recently-acquired vocabulary"; maintainers "decided against creating a universal algorithm, instead implementing user-adjustable parameters" (fetched) — [discussion #191](https://github.com/mortii/anki-morphs/discussions/191)
- Card-handling options: "Skip cards that have no unknown morphs" (with fresh-morph sub-options), "Skip cards that have unknown morphs already seen today"; recalc can suspend/move new cards "if all morphs are known (excluding fresh)" or "known or fresh"; "Shift cards" offsets due dates by "how many unknown morphs to target" (fetched) — [card handling docs](https://github.com/mortii/anki-morphs/blob/main/docs/src/user_guide/setup/settings/card_handling.md)
- AnkiMorphs settings expose weights W_total^all, W_total^unknown, W_total^learning, W_average^all, W_average^learning (in W_P) and W_target^all, W_target^learning (in W_D); "disable all the weights except for W_total^unknown" to "sort the cards based on the order of a priority file" (fetched) — [algorithm settings](https://github.com/mortii/anki-morphs/blob/main/docs/src/user_guide/setup/settings/algorithm.md)
- Known bug class: "Anki-morphs keeps showing cards with all known morphs" (issue #228) — illustrates that the known-set must be recomputed from the scheduler state on every session (fetched listing) — [issue #228](https://github.com/mortii/anki-morphs/issues/228)
- (snippet) jpdb: "Since jpdb tracks which words you've learned, it can intelligently show only sentences where you already know all the words except the word you want to look up" — [jpdb.io](https://jpdb.io/)
- (snippet) Migaku: "every 'next' flashcard contains only one new word" — [Migaku](https://migaku.com/learn-english)
- Community sentence-mining tools found on GitHub do known-word highlighting and card creation, not selection: Bunmine "can search Japanese subtitles, highlight known words and attach sentence audio"; an `anki_vocab.py` script "pick words + n+1 sentences" from subtitles using jisho.org/jpdb.io; `anki_miner` maintains a `known_word_db` synced with Anki (fetched listings) — [bunmine](https://github.com/eulerformula69/bunmine); [KayoticOrder dotfiles anki-vocab-builder](https://github.com/KayoticOrder/dotfiles); [anki_miner](https://github.com/0xzerolight/anki_miner)
- (snippet, title only) Academic framework for choosing corpus sentences as exercise items: Pilán, Volodina et al., "Candidate sentence selection for language learning exercises: from a comprehensive framework to an empirical evaluation" (2017), and "Detecting Context Dependence in Exercise Item Candidates Selected from Corpora" (2016) — [arXiv 1706.03530](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.03530); [arXiv 1605.01845](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.01845)
- Passage-level learner-independent difficulty: Lee & Hasebe formula (see Q1) — [jreadability](https://github.com/joshdavham/jreadability)

### Inferences
- **Generalise AnkiMorphs to probabilistic knowledge and multiple node types.** Replace |M_U| with the expected number of unknown *components* in the sentence: `E[U(s)] = Σ_{c ∈ comps(s), c ≠ target} (1 − P_known(c))`, where comps(s) includes lexeme-senses, kanji readings, and grammar forms parsed from the sentence. A sentence is "i+1 for target t" when `P_known(t) < τ_low` and `E[U(s) \ {t}] < ε` (e.g., 0.25) *and* no single non-target component has `P_known < 0.5` (guards against a single very-unknown node hiding behind an averaged expectation). Use the `Σ (1−P)` form for ranking and the min-P guard for eligibility.
- **Pain-node density.** Define pain nodes as components with recent failures or high confusion mass; add a bonus term `Σ_{c ∈ comps(s)} pain(c)` where `pain(c) = P_known(c) · (1 − P_known(c)) · recency_weight` (reward sentences that re-expose uncertain-but-probably-known nodes — this is the analogue of AnkiMorphs' "learning morphs" preference), capped so it never outranks the 1-unknown constraint (the same `min(·, PU−1)` trick).
- **Diversity.** After scoring, select greedily with a submodular penalty on repeated non-target components and repeated syntactic frames (Jaccard over comps(s) and over grammar-form ids) so that ten consecutive sentences don't all contain the same known collocation; jpdb-style banks (10⁸ sentences) make this cheap because candidates per target are pre-indexed by target id.
- **Grammar as first-class unknowns.** AnkiMorphs treats only morphs; for Japanese, a conjugation/construction node (e.g., 〜てしまう, causative-passive) must count toward U(s) with its own P_known, otherwise "one unknown word" sentences smuggle in unknown grammar. HLR's tagged-lexeme features are the precedent for parsing forms into (lemma, form-tags).
- **Index design (device-local).** Precompute for each bank sentence: `comp_ids[]` (sorted int ids), `len`, `readability`, `frame_hash`. Per query: candidate set = inverted index by target id; filter by `Σ(1−P)` using a cached `P_known` array indexed by comp id (recomputed after each evidence batch); this is O(candidates × avg comps) with no ML at query time.

- No open-source "Japanese i+1 sentence selector" repository beyond AnkiMorphs/MorphMan was found via GitHub search with several phrasings; the Pilán et al. selection framework could not be read.

## Key question 5 — Confusion modelling: tracking confusable pairs (歩↔足, 大変↔難しい), detection and remediation

### Takeaway
There is emerging research (LECTOR, 2025) that builds an LLM-derived pairwise semantic-similarity/confusion matrix over concepts and uses it inside a spaced-repetition scheduler, and interleaving research supports contrastive presentation for discrimination; the Japanese tool ecosystem has ready-made *static* confusable graphs (visually similar kanji from shared KRADFILE radicals/WaniKani lists; homonyms/synonyms via niai). No fetched source describes a per-learner, evidence-driven confusion-pair model with detection from error patterns; that must be designed (below).

### Cited Findings
- (snippet) LECTOR: "existing algorithms often struggle with semantic interference"; it "leverages large language models for semantic analysis while incorporating personalized learning profiles", builds a matrix that "captures pairwise semantic relationships and enables identification of potentially confusing concept combinations" and integrates it "with established spaced repetition principles" — [LECTOR (arXiv 2508.03275)](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03275v1)
- (snippet) Interleaved spaced repetition software (ISRS) "combines the principles of task interleaving ... and spaced repetition"; results "present a reasonable argument regarding task interleaving inclusion viability within spaced repetition systems to promote a balanced and deeper learning of vocabulary" — [CALL-EJ: Effects of Interleaved Spaced Repetition Learning on Vocabulary Knowledge](https://callej.org/index.php/journal/article/view/87)
- (snippet) Spacing and interleaving "require distinct theoretical bases": a systematic review tests the cognitive-load vs discriminative-contrast hypotheses — interleaving's benefit is attributed to discriminative contrast between confusable categories — [Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09613-w)
- Static confusable data for Japanese: `kanji-data` computes a "confusables: visually similar kanji (computed from shared radicals)" column from KRADFILE (EDRDG kanji → radical decomposition) alongside on/kun readings, frequency, JLPT, WaniKani level (fetched) — [bagustris/kanji-data](https://github.com/bagustris/kanji-data); niai aggregates "similar Kanjis, Homonyms, Synonyms" from WaniKani API, JMdict, KANJIDIC, KRADFILE/RADKFILE and the Innocent Corpus into JSON served by an API (fetched) — [mrahhal/niai](https://github.com/mrahhal/niai); WaniKani exposes a "visually similar kanji" relation used by userscripts (fetched listing) — [Kumirei Userscripts](https://github.com/Kumirei/Userscripts); apps build "similar-kanji sessions" from niai data (fetched) — [Kakehashi](https://github.com/Portego-00/Kakehashi)
- Ebisu's fuzzy quiz supports partial-credit results (0 ≤ result ≤ 1), which can carry "correct meaning, wrong reading" style graded outcomes (fetched) — [fasiha/ebisu](https://github.com/fasiha/ebisu)
- Bunki codex already defines `ConfusionSet` ("Similar meanings, forms, readings, or usage boundaries"), edge `sense CONTRASTS_WITH sense`, and capability label 8 "Discrimination: separation from a named confusable" (local) — [codex §4.2–4.3, §5.2](/home/user/Bunki-app/docs/convergence/JAPANESE_LEARNING_OS_CODEX_V1_FREEZE_2026-07-27.md)

### Inferences
- **Detection from the ledger, not from priors alone.** Record the learner's *actual response* (not just correct/incorrect) for every Tier-A/B event. A confusion edge (a→b) is instantiated when response = b's form/reading/meaning while target = a, or when the learner marks a as "known" but fails a's discrimination probe. Maintain per-edge counts `(conf_ab, trials_a)` and an estimated confusion probability `π_ab = (conf_ab + κ·prior_ab) / (trials_a + κ)` where `prior_ab` comes from static similarity (shared radicals / shared reading / LLM semantic similarity as in LECTOR) and κ is a pseudo-count (e.g., 2). This is the "ghost counts" trick applied to edges.
- **Effect on the frontier.** A node's effective P_known for *selection* should be discounted by its outgoing confusion mass: `P_eff(a) = P_known(a) · Π_b (1 − π_ab)`; nodes with high π become pain nodes and get contrastive treatment rather than more of the same drill.
- **Remediation policy (from the interleaving literature).** When π_ab > threshold (e.g., 0.25 with ≥3 trials), schedule a contrastive block: interleaved minimal-pair items (same sentence frame, a vs b), forced-choice discrimination with the confusable as distractor, and then i+1 sentences containing a where b would be plausible. Log discrimination events as their own contract (label 8) so a's recall stability isn't polluted by b-confusions.
- **Pairs like 大変↔難しい are semantic, 歩↔足 are visual/reading; store the edge `kind` (visual, reading, semantic, usage) because the remediation template differs (component highlighting vs meaning contrast).

- No source found that quantifies how often distractor-driven confusions occur in Japanese learners' recall, or gives thresholds for when to trigger contrastive drills.
- The interleaving papers concern vocabulary categories, not kanji; transfer to kanji-visual confusions is inference.

## Key question 6 — Kanji readings as nodes: modelling 音読み/訓読み per kanji per word, and error propagation

### Takeaway
The data needed to make readings first-class nodes exists and is open: JmdictFurigana maps each kanji span in ~178k JMdict headwords to its reading, KANJIDIC supplies per-kanji on/kun inventories, and KRADFILE supplies components. WaniKani is the shipping precedent for testing a kanji's reading separately from its meaning and for testing vocabulary readings separately again; Satori Reader is the precedent for a per-kanji known-set driving furigana. No research was found on how reading errors should propagate; a factor-graph style rule is proposed.

### Cited Findings
- JmdictFurigana: maps "each kanji character (or character span) within Japanese dictionary words to its phonetic reading"; text format `<kanji>|<kana>|<furigana string>`, e.g. `大人買い|おとながい|0-1:おとな;2:が` (span 0–1 reads おとな, char 2 reads が); coverage 177,770 of 234,814 JMdict entries and 584,141 of 741,346 JMnedict entries; "not a lexical parser", accuracy "not 100%", worse on proper names (fetched) — [Doublevil/JmdictFurigana](https://github.com/Doublevil/JmdictFurigana)
- Per-kanji metadata with on'yomi/kun'yomi, JLPT, frequency, radicals (KRADFILE) is bundled in open datasets (fetched) — [bagustris/kanji-data](https://github.com/bagustris/kanji-data); niai indexes homonyms from JMdict/KANJIDIC (fetched) — [mrahhal/niai](https://github.com/mrahhal/niai)
- jpdb's API returns per-token `furigana` and vocabulary `reading`, `spelling`, `pitch_accent` — i.e., jpdb models (spelling, reading) pairs as distinct ids (`vid`, `sid`; client comments also mention a reading id) (fetched) — [janki jpdb.py](https://github.com/diemonster/janki/blob/main/src/japanese_anki/jpdb.py)
- WaniKani: reading and meaning are separate answers for each kanji and each vocabulary item; failures on either count toward the stage penalty (fetched secondary) — [Kakehashi research note](https://github.com/Portego-00/Kakehashi/blob/main/research/wanikani-custom-srs-web.md)

- (snippet) General reading heuristics: "compound words (two or more kanji put together) use the on'yomi readings, while kanji with hiragana attached use the kun'yomi readings" — [WaniKani knowledge base](https://knowledge.wanikani.com/wanikani/japanese/onyomi-kunyomi/); [Tofugu](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese/onyomi-kunyomi/)
- Bunki codex defines `Reading` ("Reading linked to character, lexeme, and actual usage"), `Component`, and edge `component MAY_FUNCTION_AS_PHONETIC_IN kanji, only when sourced` (local) — [codex §4.2–4.3](/home/user/Bunki-app/docs/convergence/JAPANESE_LEARNING_OS_CODEX_V1_FREEZE_2026-07-27.md)

### Inferences
- **Node types.** `KanjiReading(kanji, reading, kind ∈ {on, kun, nanori, irregular})` and `WordReadingSlot(lexeme, span, KanjiReading)` derived from JmdictFurigana. A word's reading contract decomposes into its slots plus an `irregular/jukujikun` flag when the furigana string cannot be aligned per kanji (e.g., `0-1:おとな` spans two kanji → treat 大人 as an atomic reading node, not as 大=おと + 人=な).
- **Propagation rule (factor graph with noisy-AND).** P(read word w correctly) ≈ P(lexeme w recognised) · Π_slots P(slot reading known) with a leak (guess) term. On a *correct* reading of w, apply a positive update to every slot's KanjiReading node with weight 1/|slots| (or weighted by how surprising each slot is); on an *incorrect* reading where the learner's answer is recorded, attribute the failure to the slot(s) whose kana differ (e.g., 歩道 read as あゆみち → penalise 歩=ホ and 道=ドウ nodes; read as ほみち → penalise only 道=ドウ). This is why the ledger must store the learner's answer string. If the answer is unavailable, distribute the negative update proportionally to each slot's current uncertainty (higher-variance slots absorb more blame), following the BKT/G-UKT intuition that uncertain nodes are the likely cause.
- **Cross-word inference is the payoff.** A KanjiReading node aggregates evidence across all words using it; P_known(歩=ホ) after 歩道, 散歩, 進歩 gives a prior for the unseen 徒歩. This is where a few hundred events place tens of thousands of nodes, and it should feed the frontier (unseen words whose slots are all known are "probably readable", a distinct frontier band from "meaning unknown").
- **Rendering.** Reuse Satori's rule but per *slot* rather than per kanji: show furigana only over slots with P_known below the display threshold; this doubles as a Tier-C observation channel (user toggles furigana → weak negative).

## Key question 7 — Frontier computation with uncertainty; avoiding cold-start and calibration traps (placement: adaptive vocabulary-size tests, JLPT-style staircase, yes/no with pseudowords)

### Takeaway
"Frontier" is not defined by any fetched system; the closest shipping notions are AnkiMorphs' unknown/learning/known partition by interval and jpdb's coverage. The research pieces that make a principled frontier possible are: explicit per-node posterior uncertainty (Beta in Ebisu, Gaussian in G-UKT), attempt-count-based uncertainty in Elo, and pseudo-count priors for cold start. For placement, frequency-band vocabulary-size tests are the standard: yes/no self-report with pseudowords is cheap and its false-alarm rate correlates >0.80 with real-word over-claiming (so it is a valid correction signal), item information peaks at different frequency bands for different proficiencies (basis for adaptive band sampling), and a Japanese BCCWJ-based adaptive test claims an estimate from ~50 items. A device-local design can combine a frequency-rank prior (Elo-style item difficulty = f(frequency rank)) with a short adaptive yes/no+pseudoword placement followed by Tier-A verification probes near the estimated boundary.

### Inferences — proposed device-local design (concrete)
**Ledger (append-only, device-owned):**
```
EvidenceEvent {
  id, ts, contract_id, component_ids[],      // what was measured (may be several)
  method,            // typed_reading | mc4 | cloze | discrimination | rubric_prod | llm_judged | lookup | exposure | self_report
  tier,              // A | B | C | D  (derived from method + rubric version)
  result,            // 0..1 (binary for A; graded for B/C)
  response_text,     // learner's actual answer (needed for confusion + reading attribution)
  latency_ms, hints, context_ref (sentence/passage id), model_version
}
```
**Derived per-node state (recomputable from ledger + versioned params):**
```
NodeState {
  node_id, kind,                          // lexeme_sense | kanji_reading | grammar_form | expression | confusion_edge
  contracts: { receptive_recog, reading_retrieval, meaning_recall, productive } -> {
      fsrs: {S, D, last_review, reps, lapses}      // only from Tier-A scheduled events
      belief: {alpha, beta, t_ref}                  // Ebisu-style Beta over recall at t_ref, updated by all tiers via (q0,q1)
      n_direct, n_indirect                          // evidence counts (Elo-style uncertainty proxy)
  },
  prior_logit,                            // from frequency rank / JLPT band / placement (cold start)
  propagated_evidence: Σ weighted neighbour updates (kanji-reading slots, grammar forms, confusion mass)
}
```
**P_known and uncertainty.** `P_known = E[Beta]` at now (Ebisu decay), or FSRS R when a Tier-A state exists (prefer FSRS for R, Beta for the credible interval); `conf = alpha + beta` (pseudo-count) with a cap; `var = αβ/((α+β)²(α+β+1))`.
**Frontier definition.** Frontier(t) = nodes with `P_known ∈ [0.35, 0.75]` (uncertain), plus nodes with `P_known < 0.35` but `prior_logit` high (frequency says "next"), plus unseen nodes all of whose prerequisites/slots have P_known > 0.8 ("probably readable"). Report each frontier node with `(P_known, conf, source: direct|propagated|prior)` so the open learner model can show why. Nodes with `conf < 3` are "unplaced" and eligible for a cheap placement probe rather than a full drill.
**Cold start (placement).** (1) Frequency-band yes/no self-report over ~60 items with ~20 matched pseudowords; correct with the false-alarm rate (signal-detection style) and derive a band-wise P(known | rank) logistic curve; (2) set `prior_logit(node) = a − b·log(rank)` from that curve; (3) immediately verify with 20–30 Tier-A probes in the estimated boundary band (reading + meaning) to calibrate `q0` for the self-report channel; (4) treat all placement-derived P as `conf ≤ 2` so any direct event dominates. JLPT-band staircase can seed grammar-form priors the same way (band-wise yes/no on constructions, then probes).
**Calibration traps to encode.** Never display or select on placement-only P as if it were verified; track calibration by binning predicted P vs Tier-A outcomes (FSRS's RMSE(bins) metric) per tier and per method; shrink tier weights whose bins miscalibrate.

## Key question 8 — Open learner models: showing learners their own model, correction affordances, effects on motivation

### Takeaway
Two recent reviews frame OLMs along a continuum — Inspectable → Negotiable → Editable → Persuasive/Adaptive — with increasing learner agency; inspectable models mainly support monitoring, negotiable ones support calibration via dialogue, editable ones support autonomy and goal setting, and adaptive ones personalise scaffolding. Reported benefits are metacognition, self-regulation and engagement; reported risks are presentation/granularity/control-level design difficulty, learner dependency, and accountability of AI-driven personalisation. No effect sizes for motivation were obtainable in this session.

### Cited Findings
- (snippet) Systematic literature review (Computers & Education, 2020) of OLMs supporting self-regulated learning in higher education: "OLMs encourage learners to engage with their academic progress and develop metacognitive and self-regulatory capabilities"; "OLMs support cognition and then metacognition and motivation"; challenges "include presentation, transparency, granularity, control level, and theorizing" — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131520300774)
- (snippet) Meta-synthesis of 26 studies (Frontiers in Education, 2025): "a continuum of OLMs — Inspectable, Negotiable, Editable, and Persuasive/Adaptive — that reflects progressively greater levels of learner agency, transparency, and co-regulation"; "inspectable models predominantly support monitoring and awareness; negotiable models foster active calibration and dialogic feedback; editable models strengthen autonomy and goal-setting; and adaptive models, frequently mediated by artificial intelligence, offer personalized scaffolding"; "concerns remain regarding transparency, learner dependency, and ethical accountability of AI-driven personalization" — [Frontiers in Education 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1760183/full); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/400140397_Open_learner_models_and_pedagogical_strategies_in_higher_education_a_meta-synthesis_of_approaches_to_self-regulated_learning)
- (snippet) Earlier work on OLMs to "promote active thinking by enhancing self-regulated learning in online higher education" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333461130_The_potential_of_open_learner_models_to_promote_active_thinking_by_enhancing_self-regulated_learning_in_online_higher_education_learning_environments)

### Inferences
- The frontier report from Q7 (P_known, confidence, evidence source) is an *inspectable* OLM; making it *negotiable/editable* means: (a) "I know this" / "I don't" buttons that append a `self_report` event with its own (q0,q1) rather than overwriting the state; (b) a per-node evidence trail (which sentences, which probes) so corrections are grounded; (c) a "why is this on my frontier?" explanation listing prior vs propagated vs direct contributions — the same decomposition AnkiMorphs shows in `am-score-terms`.
- The dependency/false-precision concern argues for displaying uncertainty explicitly (e.g., "probably known, low confidence: 2 observations") and for never showing a single global level, which matches the codex's "no global scalar" rule.
