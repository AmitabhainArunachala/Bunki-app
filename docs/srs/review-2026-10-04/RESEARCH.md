# SRS research and five recommended strategies

Reviewed 2026-10-04. This synthesis supersedes conflicting conclusions in the seven
earlier files under `docs/srs/research/`; those files remain historical evidence.
The learner explicitly reopened their stated MCD choices during this review.
Recommendations therefore do not assume that MCD must win.

## Recommendation

**For a reading-first goal, start with targeted meaning retrieval from Japanese,
then use MCD selectively. Keep FSRS-6.** Show the target prominently in enough
context to identify its sense. Use a word-first variant when the sense remains clear;
use context-first for ambiguous words, expressions and discourse-dependent senses.
Make additional form, listening and use tasks deliberate choices.

This ranking is a product judgment about the learner's reading-mined list and
review workload. No direct, long-term Japanese MCD-versus-TSC trial found in this
review establishes a universal winner. The robust common foundation is retrieval,
corrective feedback and spacing, including successful retrieval again on later days.
The branded card formats have much less direct comparative evidence. [R01–R06]

Evidence labels below: **E** empirical study/review; **M** method author's practice;
**D** official technical documentation; **J** our design judgment. A mechanism study
is not automatically a test of a complete app or a particular Japanese deck.

## Top five

These are complementary functions, not five mandatory cards for each of 323 words.
Listening or active-use priorities would change the order.

| Rank | Strategy                                                | Concrete Bunki behavior                                                                                                                                                                                                              | Why and limits                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ---- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | **Targeted meaning retrieval with appropriate context** | Japanese target is prominent; mentally recall its selected meaning; reveal concise sense-specific feedback. | 3    | **Selective MCD for a defined retrieval gap**           | Blank a word, partner word, particle, chunk or verified spelling unit. ### Worked examples

The following Japanese is **written illustration**, not mined quotation or a
native-editor-certified release candidate.

- **Targeted meaning:** `人口が減り、市の財政が厳しくなった。` Target 財政;
  answer “public finances / the city's finances,” not a generic statement about
  any money. A separate reading probe can ask for ざいせい.
- **Selective collocation:** `長年の調査が、研究の礎を［　］。` Cue the intended
  past-tense collocation 築いた, while checking whether alternatives are valid for
  the task. This practices the phrase, not every character independently.
- **Sense separation:** なお meaning “still” and なお introducing an additional
  note need different contextual meanings. A single “furthermore” answer cannot
  grade both reliably.
- **Rare spelling:** 軈 encountered as やがて can remain receptive vocabulary
  unless recalling the rare written form is a chosen goal. Showing kana is helpful
  for an orthography task and would reveal the answer to a reading task.
- **Transfer:** after learning 財政 in a city-budget example, test a different
  comprehensible fiscal context without the familiar layout or sentence. Record
  whether the learner understood the target, not whether the passage was familiar.

## What the evidence changes

### Retrieval, inference and rich context

The earlier synthesis overgeneralizes van den Broek et al. 2018 into a claim that
rich context helps comprehension but harms durable learning. Its intentional
practice experiments support making successful retrieval happen. The same research
line's 2022 story-reading study is a necessary boundary condition: informative
contexts outperformed retrieval contexts in the weaker-encoding experiment, and
matched retrieval plus feedback after stronger initial encoding. These were short
foreign-vocabulary experiments, not months of Japanese MCD. Preserve useful
context; do not deliberately make inference impossible on every card. [R04, R10]

Karpicke and Roediger support repeated retrieval after initial success. Successive
relearning combines criterion learning with subsequent spaced sessions. Neither
means endless immediate repetitions or maximizing card count. Nakata's study of
98 Japanese learners of English compared 1/3/5/7 within-session retrievals: more
retrievals improved raw delayed performance, while one was most efficient after
controlling time. Same-day repetition is not useless; the tradeoff is benefit per
minute. No cited experiment establishes 1m/10m as uniquely optimal for MCD. [R01–R02, R14]

### Context diversity has limits

Chen, Miller and Ke's 2026 meta-analysis reports a behavioral effect of
**d = .444, 95% CI [.329, .559]** from 12 studies/18 samples. The review covers
20 studies overall; those are not all the inputs to that estimate. Definitions of
diversity, exposure, materials and outcomes vary. This is not an expected gain from
adding a third Bunki passage, and it does not identify a contexts-per-word quota. [R07]

Norman et al. studied 239 participants learning pseudowords through reading.
Diversity benefited generalization of meanings to unfamiliar contexts; effects
did not simply generalize to every word-form outcome. Familiar-context testing
can favor repeated context. Mak et al.'s anchoring work supports considering the
order of familiar and varied encounters, not FSRS stability thresholds of 3 or
14 days. The Mak item was verified at abstract level, not full text. [R08–R09]

### One task is not one unknown word or 98% coverage

AJATT's original examples focus one scored gap while retaining useful surrounding
context; they include character, chunk, word and particle gaps, both short and long
text, and bilingual material. Khatz's later format separates reading and character
objectives. The “never two kanji” Part 3 byline is **rigabamboo**, not Khatzumoto.
These are method descriptions, not controlled evidence for a universal granularity
rule. The earlier report cannot claim all communities converge on recognition-first. [R03]

TSC's one-target principle is a task design aid. One unfamiliar item in ten tokens
is 90% coverage; one in fifty is 98%. Corpus-level coverage estimates do not certify
that a particular learner knows a given sentence's background words. JLPT and
frequency lists are proxies, not personal knowledge measurements. Avoid both an
unreadable context and an artificial rule that every non-target word must be known. [R05, J]

### Production, interleaving and mnemonics

Webb's receptive/productive comparison supports outcome-specific practice: a task
that teaches recognizing meaning need not teach spelling or use equally. It used
English learning and word-pair conditions, not Japanese MCD versus TSC. [R06]

The authors also suggest productive learning may be more effective if only one
method is used, considering its broader gains. That is a counterweight to choosing
receptive work alone. Our provisional default targets reading meaning specifically
and keeps productive practice available; it is not the paper's universal recommendation.

Nakata and Suzuki's grammar study involved 115 Japanese learners and found a
delayed advantage for interleaving English grammar structures. Their vocabulary
study enrolled 133 (122 analyzed) and found more interference for semantically
related items without an overall relatedness advantage. “Interleave” does not
mean learning a list of near-synonyms together from scratch. [R12–R13]

Dunlosky et al.'s review supports testing and distributed practice broadly; keyword
mnemonic benefits have narrower conditions and costs. Optional personal imagery or
stories can be useful. A primitive decomposition is not automatically etymology,
and no cited study establishes that eight themes, texture or card-edge hue improves
Japanese retention. [R15]

## Other ways to schedule SRS

Card format, interval scheduling, task selection and study habits are separate
decisions. Fixing one does not establish that the others are correct.

Benchmark log loss/AUC is not randomized evidence of a particular Japanese learning gain. Clean task evidence is prerequisite to fitting. [R16–R17]          |

| HLR                     | Duolingo's feature-based half-life regression. The 2016 paper studies prediction and an engagement experiment.                                         | Consider task/linguistic features in research. | ACT-R adaptive practice | Activation from frequency, recency and spacing; can incorporate latency.                                                                               | Serious cognitive alternative with paired-associate scheduling experiments; needs task-specific assumptions and data. A benchmark implementation is not the whole ACT-R family. [R21] |
| SSP-MMC / Plus          | Memory model plus stochastic shortest-path optimization of expected review cost.                                                                       | Useful optimal-control comparator. | MEMORIZE / SELECT       | Stochastic control; MEMORIZE evaluates a Duolingo natural experiment, SELECT includes randomized adaptive item selection for driving-permit questions. | Evidence that selection policies can matter, with different materials/comparators. | Ebisu                   | Bayesian memory-probability and half-life updates.                                                                                                     | Useful uncertainty-oriented framework; no demonstrated advantage for this deployment. [R25]                                                                                           |
| Neural history models   | RNN/LSTM/Transformer predictors evaluated in review-log benchmarks.                                                                                    | Potential research comparators, with larger data/deployment costs. Predictive accuracy alone does not demonstrate better transfer. [R17]                                              |

Personalized classroom review also has evidence from Lindsey et al.'s Spanish study:
the abstract reports 16.5% improvement over massed review and 10.0% over generic
spaced review. These are not percentage-point claims and are not FSRS effect sizes.
Do not promise the learner these gains. [R26]

Retain the existing .90 target and short steps as compatible starting settings.
Higher retention has workload costs; format labels alone do not justify .95 or
separate fitted models. Test calibration on held-out, correctly labelled reviews
before tuning. The wrapper currently corrupts that prerequisite through mixed
tasks, missing assistance and lost history. [R16; REVIEW findings F01–F08]

## A useful personal comparison

After the release blockers are repaired, compare matched, disjoint word/sense
families under targeted meaning versus selective MCD. Match baseline familiarity,
frequency, length, register and difficulty; use comparable review time, not merely
equal card counts. Studying the same word in both arms contaminates the comparison.

Measure delayed meaning and reading separately in held-out natural contexts,
review minutes, assistance, ambiguity, lapses, enjoyment and abandonment. Add audio
or use probes only if those are goals. Approximately 7- and 28-day probes are
practical design choices, not scientifically privileged intervals. Record outside
exposure, missing sessions and initial failures. Small personal results guide this
learner; they do not establish a universal population effect. Do not optimize for
familiar-card pass rate or ask AI to infer the learner's grades.
