# Anchor each word in one ranked native sentence

Build the 323-word deck as **one Targeted Sentence Card (TSC) per word sense**. The front shows a single native sentence with the target word bolded and no furigana on the target, and the learner grades only on that word. Choose the sentence with a GDEX-style filter-and-rank pipeline over openly licensed corpora. If the learner's own looked-up sentence exists, prefer it. Add a second or third sentence from a _different_ register only for high-value words, and only after the first card has stabilised. Every tradition surveyed converges on this default. AJATT, Tatsumoto, TheMoeWay/Lapis, Kaishi and Tango all use one native sentence per word, recognition-first, with one thing tested per card. The lexicography research (GDEX, HitEx, Benedetti et al.'s Japanese system) shows sentence choice can be automated reliably. Three constraints shape the build. First, only **Tatoeba (CC BY 2.0 FR)** and **Aozora Bunko (public domain)** clear both licensing and GitHub-only access. Second, Tatoeba has **zero hits for words like 遡及, 慮る and ぎくしゃく**. Third, a sizeable minority of the list (rare N1 terms, onomatopoeia, slang) will therefore need originally written sentences, held to a stricter verification bar. On scheduling, run FSRS at about **0.90 desired retention** with **Again/Good grading**. Introduce cloze or production cards only as gated, later siblings, because the evidence says retrieval drives retention while context mainly drives comprehension.

## Every major method prescribes one native sentence and one target per card

The communities disagree loudly but agree on more than they admit. Khatzumoto's original 10,000 Sentences method used a bare native sentence on the front, with a reading and definition on the back. It explicitly rejected English-to-Japanese production: "Good Japanese starts with mindlessly imitating good Japanese. Don't go inventing your own Japanese" ([AJATT, 10,000 Sentences: How](https://github.com/all-japanese-all-the-time/all-japanese-all-the-time/tree/master/blog/10000-sentences-how)). Its length rule was strict: a **hard upper limit of 30 characters, with most items 10–15**. Long sentences were trimmed at commas or particles, and the full original was kept on the answer side ([AJATT, Popping Bubblewrap](https://github.com/all-japanese-all-the-time/all-japanese-all-the-time/tree/master/blog/popping-bubblewrap-tips-for-better-srs-sentence-items)). Khatz's own sample card is close to this user's vocabulary profile. The front is a news-register clause containing 熾烈. The back is only the dictionary entry for 熾烈, with no translation ([AJATT, Real Sample Sentences](https://github.com/all-japanese-all-the-time/all-japanese-all-the-time/tree/master/blog/real-sample-sentences)).

In 2010 Khatz killed roughly 15,000 sentence cards in favour of **Massive-Context Cloze Deletions (MCDs)**. An MCD keeps a large native passage on the front and blanks one kanji, kana chunk or particle. The principle: "instead of having 1 card with 9 unknowns, you have 9 cards with 1 unknown each" ([AJATT, 12 Free MCD Examples](https://github.com/all-japanese-all-the-time/all-japanese-all-the-time/tree/master/blog/12-free-mcd-examples); [AJATT, 10,000 Sentences is Dead](https://github.com/all-japanese-all-the-time/all-japanese-all-the-time/tree/master/blog/10000-sentences-is-dead-long-live-mcds)). The two eras disagree on length but share one invariant: **test exactly one thing**. AJATT also pairs sentence collection with ruthless pruning: "If in doubt, throw it out", but "Total deletion bad" ([AJATT, If In Doubt](https://github.com/all-japanese-all-the-time/all-japanese-all-the-time/tree/master/blog/srs-if-in-doubt-throw-it-out-ambivalence-is-the-greatest-enemy); [AJATT, How Not To Delete Cards](https://github.com/all-japanese-all-the-time/all-japanese-all-the-time/tree/master/blog/srs-how-not-to-delete-cards)).

Tatsumoto, AJATT's most detailed successor, formalised the **Targeted Sentence Card**. The target word is highlighted, and "if you accidentally forget some part that is not highlighted, it shouldn't be taken into account when grading." The back needs only the definition, and a sentence translation is optional ([Tatsumoto, card templates](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/discussing-various-card-templates.html)). He claims TSCs review **up to 2–4× faster** than whole-sentence cards. He prefers "1T" sentences with exactly one unknown, ideally "the original sentence you found the word in." He substitutes from a sentence bank when that sentence has several unknowns, and makes "separate cards for other meanings when it's necessary" ([Tatsumoto, Sentence mining](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/sentence-mining.html); [One target sentences](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/one-target-sentences.html)). He forbids furigana on the front, which he says stops you learning readings. He also recommends kanjifying kana words that have a kanji spelling ([furigana post](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/i-forget-kanji-readings-in-sentences-should-i-use-furigana.html); [kana words post](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/how-should-i-make-cards-for-japanese-words-that-dont-have-any-kanji.html)). He is openly sceptical of cloze: "there is always an infinite number of answers for any given 'fill in the blank' question… cloze cards stop working once the intervals grow big enough" ([Tatsumoto, on MCDs](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/what-do-you-think-of-using-mcds-to-train-production.html)).

The current TheMoeWay/kuri ecosystem moved the other way, toward **word-front cards with the sentence as a hint**. This descends from Animecards and is implemented as per-card toggles in the Lapis note type. `IsWordAndSentenceCard` shows the sentence beneath the word, `IsClickCard` reveals it on click, `IsSentenceCard` makes a TSC, and `IsAudioCard` makes an audio cloze ([Lapis README](https://github.com/donkuri/lapis)). Shoui keeps the sentence on the front only as "visual context" and grades on meaning, then reading, with Pass/Fail. He runs FSRS at 80–95% retention and about 20 new cards a day ([TheMoeWay, Shoui method](https://learnjapanese.moe/shouimethod/)). JPMN's hover and click hybrids exist explicitly "to prevent context-based memories". These are the memories you form when you recognise a sentence instead of the word ([JPMN card types](https://github.com/Aquafina-water-bottle/jp-mining-note/blob/master/docs/docs/cardtypes.md)). Lapis lists the uses of a hint field: disambiguating readings, words that appear only in fixed contexts, onomatopoeia and kana words, and rescuing repeatedly failed cards ([Lapis README](https://github.com/donkuri/lapis)). Refold's pages were unreachable. Search snippets indicate the same 1T mining with a conservative **5–10 new cards per day** and FSRS, but those specifics are unverified ([Refold, Learning words with Anki](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/learning-words-with-anki)).

The premade decks show what curation looks like in practice. **Kaishi 1.5k** merged Core 2k/10k and Tango N4/N5. It sorted words by frequency, "chose the best sentence for each word," and fixed about **120 of 1,500 sentences (8%)**. It shows the word plus the sentence with the word bolded on the front. Its authors criticised Core 2k for "mistranslations… sometimes not even reflecting the meaning of the word used" ([Kaishi README](https://github.com/donkuri/Kaishi)). Tatsumoto rates **Tango (Ankidrone Essentials)** above Core because its sentences are "a little more natural" and roughly ordered 1T. He considers Core 10k better used as a sentence bank than studied in full ([Ankidrone Essentials](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/ankidrone-essentials.html); [on Core 6k](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/what-do-you-think-of-the-core-6k-deck.html)).

None of these communities endorses AI-written sentences. Khatz would not even trust lightly edited human corpora: "when sentences are your primary learning medium, you need to be able to trust what you read 100%" ([AJATT, 10,000 Sentences: Where](https://github.com/all-japanese-all-the-time/all-japanese-all-the-time/tree/master/blog/10000-sentences-where)). Tatsumoto concedes that modern LLMs can be "quite accurate" but warns that learners cannot verify them ([Tatsumoto on AI](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/have-you-ever-tried-using-ai-to-analyze-grammar.html)). Kaishi disclaims all AI modifications. Any original-sentence fallback therefore has to earn trust through labelling and verification, a point developed below.

## A Japanese GDEX pipeline can rank candidates automatically

The lexicography literature has solved most of the "pick a good example" problem with a simple architecture: **hard filters, then a product of soft penalties**. GDEX (Kilgarriff et al. 2008) operationalises the Atkins and Rundell criteria of _typical_, _intelligible_ and _informative_. It filters on whole-sentence form, length bounds, illegal characters and a frequency floor for every word. It then penalises rare characters, anaphoric pronouns, sensitive words, abbreviations and conjunction-initial sentences ([GDEX 2008](https://www.sketchengine.eu/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/GDEX_Automatically_finding_2008.pdf); [Kosem et al. 2019](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327163304_Identification_and_automatic_extraction_of_good_dictionary_examples_The_cases_of_GDEX)). The published Slovene pedagogical configuration is concrete enough to port. It gives 50 points for passing all hard filters (whole sentence, 5 < length < 20 tokens, no illegal characters, every word above a frequency floor). Up to 50 more come from `optimal_interval(length, 10, 12)` multiplied by greylist penalties: 0.05 per rare character, 0.1 per anaphor, 0.25 per bad word, 0.5 per abbreviation, and a halving for a sentence-initial conjunction ([Sketch Engine GDEX syntax](https://www.sketchengine.eu/syntax-of-gdex-configuration-files/)). Germany's DWDS used similar rules and judged **95.3% of extracted examples acceptable** ([Euralex 2012](https://euralex.org/publications/automatic-example-sentence-extraction-for-a-contemporary-german-dictionary/)). Swedish HitEx added a CEFR-level classifier, a co-occurrence typicality ranker and an explicit **context-dependence detector** for sentences that only make sense with their neighbours ([Pilán et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03530); [context dependence](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.01845)).

For Japanese specifically, the strongest evidence is **Benedetti et al. (ACL SRW 2024)**. They retrieved candidates from a JLPT-labelled web corpus and ranked them by three things: closeness to a target JLPT level (via a BERT classifier), contextual sense similarity of the target word, and a diversity-maximising final selection. **Learners, native speakers and GPT-4 all preferred retrieved sentences over LLM-generated ones** ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-srw.55/); [arXiv 2506.03580](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03580)). That result cuts against an L2 Spanish study in which learners preferred GenAI examples 265 to 10. That study traced the preference to the roughly 30% of corpus sentences that were not comprehensible out of context ([NLP4CALL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.nlp4call-1.3.pdf)). The two findings reconcile: corpus sentences win _if_ context-dependent ones are filtered out. HitEx's context-dependence check is therefore one of the most important filters in the pipeline.

Difficulty can be estimated with off-the-shelf Japanese resources. **jReadability** gives a linear score from mean sentence length and the proportions of kango, wago, verbs and particles (0.5–6.4, higher is easier). Its companion **日本語教育語彙表** grades about **17,920 words into six teacher-judged levels**. That list has become a de facto difficulty standard since the post-2010 JLPT stopped publishing word lists ([jreadability](https://pypi.org/project/jreadability/1.1.4/); [JEV](https://jhlee.sakura.ne.jp/JEV/)). jReadability was built for texts and is noisy on single sentences. Per-word level counts are the sharper sentence-level signal. For the i+1 target, coverage research supports roughly **98% known words** for unassisted comprehension ([Schmitt et al. 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf)). At sentence scale that means one unknown content word: Tatsumoto's 1T rule restated.

**Recommendation (a): how to mine and rank sentences for each word.** First, use the sentence where the learner actually looked the word up, if the app captured one. It is personally anchored, which Tatsumoto argues "greatly aids memorization", and it shows the sense the learner actually needs. Otherwise, morphologically analyse each candidate (Sudachi or MeCab with UniDic) and match on _lemma_, not substring, so inflected verbs and adjectives are found. Then apply hard filters:

- The sentence is complete: it ends in 。！？ or a closing 」 and contains a predicate.
- The target appears as the intended part of speech, not inside a longer compound.
- There are no URLs, emoji, half-width kana, unmatched brackets, or old kana such as ゐ/ゑ.
- The sentence does not start with a context-dependent connective (しかし, だから, それで, 一方, また).
- It has no unresolved こそあど demonstrative or 彼/彼女 as the subject.
- Length is about **12–45 characters**. That is longer than AJATT's beginner limit of 30, because N2–N1 news and academic words need a clause of context. It stays below the point where a review becomes a reading exercise.

Then score the survivors multiplicatively on four factors:

- **Unknown-word count:** penalise each non-target content word above the learner's level, using the JEV level or a known-word list.
- **Kanji:** penalise non-Jōyō kanji outside the target.
- **Typicality:** reward the presence of the target's top collocation frames, using logDice, the Sketch Engine association score ([Rychlý 2008](https://nlp.fi.muni.cz/raslan/2008/papers/13.pdf)), computed over case-particle pairs such as 懸念＋を＋抱く.
- **Proper nouns:** penalise them as a ranker, not a filter, following HitEx.

For polysemous words, cluster candidates by contextual embedding or by JMdict sense, and pick the top sentence of the sense the learner looked up. If a cloze card will be generated, add an answerability check. Mask the target in a Japanese masked LM and require it to rank in the top few. Horsmann and Zesch showed that ranking by ambiguity raises correct target production from **36.5% to 59.9%**, which is better but still far from perfect ([Horsmann & Zesch 2014](https://aclanthology.org/W14-3503.pdf)). Finally, put the top two or three candidates in front of the learner and let them choose or reject. The learner's veto is a cheap and powerful final filter, and Kaishi's 8% repair rate on already-curated sentences shows why one is needed.

## Tatoeba and Aozora are the only open sources that clear every bar

Most of the sentences learners actually mine come from sites an app cannot redistribute. NHK News Web Easy forbids reproduction and modification. Weblio forbids reprinting without written consent. Massif relies on "fair use" excerpts of copyrighted novels. ImmersionKit publishes no reuse licence for its anime subtitles. JParaCrawl is research-only. BCCWJ costs **¥400,000 to ¥8,000,000** for commercial use ([NHK terms via nhk-easy](https://pypi.org/project/nhk-easy/); [Weblio terms](https://www.weblio.jp/info/terms.jsp); [massif README](https://github.com/rsimmons/massif); [ImmersionKit docs](https://docs.immersionkit.com/public%20api/search/); [JParaCrawl](https://www.kecl.ntt.co.jp/icl/lirg/jparacrawl/); [BCCWJ fees](https://clrd.ninjal.ac.jp/bccwj/fee.html)). These remain useful as _lookup_ references for a human writing or checking a sentence. They should not be mined into the shipped deck.

**Tatoeba** is the workhorse. It is CC BY 2.0 FR (some sentences CC0) and bans AI-generated sentences. Its "OK" tags mark native-checked sentences ([Tatoeba downloads](https://tatoeba.org/en/downloads); [Tatoeba guidelines](https://en.wiki.tatoeba.org/articles/show/guidelines)). A GitHub mirror reachable from the build environment holds **232,778 Japanese sentences with 280,520 English links and furigana** ([Tatoeba-Rework](https://github.com/TheSunphis/Tatoeba-Rework)). Its weakness is provenance. The bulk comes from the Tanaka corpus, which students typed to meet a work requirement. EDRDG documents "large numbers of errors, both in the Japanese and English… translations did not match at all", plus unnatural "direct, hard translations of English" ([EDRDG Tanaka Corpus](https://www.edrdg.org/wiki/Tanaka_Corpus.html); [Tatoeba Tanaka edit guidelines](https://en.wiki.tatoeba.org/articles/show/tanakaedits)). This is exactly why Khatz avoided it. Prefer sentences with the WWWJDIC "checked" index flag or an OK tag. Keep sentence IDs and contributor names for per-sentence attribution.

Coverage is the binding constraint. A 16-word probe of the mirror found healthy counts for common news words: **懸念 13, 措置 12, 把握 12**. Counts thinned quickly for rarer N1 words (**是正 2, 顕著 3, 脆弱 1**) and hit **zero for 遡及, 慮る and ぎくしゃく** ([Tatoeba-Rework](https://github.com/TheSunphis/Tatoeba-Rework)). Slang fared unevenly: やばい 22, ウザい 1. That probe covered 16 words, not the full 323, so the true gap rate is unmeasured. The pattern is clear enough to plan around, though. Common N2 vocabulary will usually find a sentence. Rare N1, literary and onomatopoeic items often will not.

**Aozora Bunko** fills the literary gap. Its public-domain works may be freely copied, redistributed and adapted ([青空文庫 取り扱い規準](https://www.aozora.gr.jp/guide/kijyunn.html)). The full text set is mirrored daily at `aozorahack/aozorabunko_text` ([README](https://github.com/aozorahack/aozorabunko_text)). It needs real cleaning before use. Files are Shift_JIS, ruby appears inline as 《》 and ｜, editorial notes appear as ［＃…］, and Meiji-to-early-Shōwa texts carry old orthography and archaic usage that a modern learner should not absorb unflagged. It has no English, so translations must be written. Japanese Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 4.0) would suit academic vocabulary and JESC would suit colloquial speech. Neither has a reachable GitHub sentence extract, and JESC's licence is unverified, so both are second-tier. Mixing any CC BY-SA source in imposes share-alike on the sentence data ([Creative Commons on Wikipedia](https://creativecommons.org/2023/06/29/wikipedia-moves-to-cc-4-0-licenses/); [JESC paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10639)). SNOW T15/T23 is CC BY 4.0, but its 2,000-word simplified vocabulary rules it out for N1 ([SNOW T15](https://www.jnlp.org/GengoHouse/snow/t15)).

**Recommendation (b): when to write original sentences.** Write one only when no candidate survives the hard filters at an acceptable score. Do the same when the only survivors are context-dependent, use a different sense from the one the learner looked up, or come from Aozora in archaic usage that would mislead. For single kanji the learner looked up as a component, not as a standalone word, do not force a sentence for the bare kanji. Follow Tatsumoto's trouble-kanji method instead. Make TSCs for two common words that share the kanji, which gives a "kanji web" of real vocabulary ([Tatsumoto](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/i-forget-kanji-readings-in-sentences-should-i-use-furigana.html)). An original sentence should mimic the target's typical collocation frame and register: a news-style である sentence for 遡及, conversational for やばい. Keep it 1T, and check it against reference-only sources such as massif or a dictionary's examples. If an LLM drafts it, a native or strong-reader review and a visible provenance label ("written for this deck") are non-negotiable. The Japanese evidence favours retrieval over generation ([Benedetti et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03580)), and every community surveyed distrusts unverifiable sentences. Labelling also tells the learner which sentences to treat as models of natural Japanese and which are scaffolding.

## One anchor sentence first, then diversify only where use demands it

There is no study of "distinct sentences per word inside an SRS," so this recommendation is built from adjacent evidence. Incidental-learning research puts the number of encounters needed at anywhere from **6 to over 20**, and the meta-analytic frequency effect is a medium **r = .34** ([Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12343); [EAP Foundation summary](https://www.eapfoundation.com/vocab/learn/incidental/)). Those counts describe passive reading, though. Van den Broek et al. found that **retrieval practice beat inference from rich context for retention**, including recognition in new contexts. Context helped comprehension, not memory ([van den Broek et al. 2018](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12285)). A spaced, feedback-backed SRS review is the strongest learning event in this literature. One well-chosen card reviewed over months therefore does far more work than one incidental encounter.

What a single sentence cannot supply is breadth: collocation, register and transfer. Here the contextual-diversity research gives a consistent staged answer. A meta-analysis finds an overall benefit of diversity (**d = 0.44**) ([Applied Psycholinguistics meta-analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/effect-of-contextual-diversity-on-l1-and-l2-word-learning-a-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/56CB12725024BBE5DD57F374008F193D)). But the benefit depends on _when_. Words in diverse contexts are learned more slowly during training ([Pagán & Nation 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cogs.12705)). A preregistered N = 100 study found more semantic features recalled after a single narrative, and no diversity benefit for form ([Hulme et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-023-02316-z)). Diversity pays off _after_ a word is first anchored in a coherent context ([Mak, Hsiao & Nation 2021](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/anchoring-and-contextual-variation-in-the-early-stages-of-inciden/)). The large preregistered Norman et al. (N = 239) study showed diverse training improved generalisation to _new_ contexts, while uniform training was better in _familiar_ ones ([Norman et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/17470218221126976)). Webb adds a warning: an informative first context followed by uninformative ones can make meaning recognition _worse_ than a single encounter ([Webb 2008](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ815123.pdf)). Contextual-informativeness research found that scaffolding from highly informative to less informative contexts gave **+15% long-term retention** ([Kapelner et al.](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED589145.pdf)). Diversity benefits are also reported to grow with prior vocabulary size, which favours this N2–N1 learner ([SSLA contextual diversity](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-contextual-diversity-on-incidental-vocabulary-learning/F83A1EB55404E2E07FDC1695B0BC2BAE)).

Polysemy is a separate axis. Verspoor and Lowie showed that cueing the **core sense** improved guessing and long-term retention of figurative senses ([Verspoor & Lowie 2003](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9922.00234)). Different senses are therefore separate form–meaning mappings, each needing its own anchor, and do not count as "diversity." Collocation learning grows with exposure, but the dose is contested: about 15 exposures in one study, a plateau at 4 in another ([Pellicer-Sánchez 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168815618428)). Choosing later sentences to show _different_ high-logDice collocates is therefore the most efficient way to spend extra cards.

**Recommendation (c): how many sentences per word.** Start every word with **one anchor sentence**. It should be highly informative, use the typical collocation, and show the sense the learner looked up. Add more by tier, as a heuristic the evidence supports in direction but not in exact numbers:

- **2–3 sentences** for high-value news and academic words the learner wants to _use_, such as 懸念 or 措置. Draw the extras from a different register or genre, each showing a different collocate.
- **1, optionally 2**, for mid-frequency recognition words.
- **Exactly 1, recognition-only**, for rare and literary words.
- **1**, with an extra sentence only if it shows a genuinely different usage, for onomatopoeia and slang. These live in fixed contexts, where the Lapis-style hint is often more useful than more sentences.
- **One anchor per looked-up sense** for polysemous words, with the core meaning shown on the back of extended-sense cards.

Unlock the second sentence only after the anchor card reaches about **2–3 weeks of stability**. Also unlock it when the anchor becomes a leech, because a fresh context is the natural repair. Never introduce several sentences for the same word on the same day. Cards for the same lemma cue each other, inflate success, and mislead FSRS, which models each card independently. Group cards by lemma and enforce a minimum gap between same-lemma reviews, the app-level equivalent of FSRS Helper's "Disperse Siblings" ([FSRS Helper](https://ankiweb.net/shared/info/759844606)). For 323 words, this policy yields roughly **400–500 cards** rather than 1,000. At 10–20 new cards a day, the whole list is introduced in about three to six weeks.

## Recognition TSCs carry the deck; production is a gated sibling

The SLA evidence maps cleanly onto the community split. Webb found **receptive learning gave larger gains in receptive meaning knowledge**, while productive learning gave broader gains in orthography, productive meaning and syntax ([Webb 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688209343854)). Nakata found recall formats beat recognition when orthographic form must be produced ([Nakata 2016](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)). He also argued for sequencing formats from easier to harder retrieval ([Nakata 2011](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254217121_Computer-assisted_second_language_vocabulary_learning_in_a_paired-associate_paradigm_A_critical_investigation_of_flashcard_software)). With corrective feedback, short-answer recall outperformed multiple choice ([Kang, McDermott & Roediger 2007](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/test-format-and-corrective-feedback-modify-the-effect-of-testing-/)). A learner who mined these words from news and reading needs receptive meaning and reading first. That is exactly what a TSC trains. Cloze earns its place for words the learner wants to write or say. Tatsumoto's objection applies, though: an open blank has many acceptable fillers, so a cloze should test a constrained collocation (懸念を**抱く**) or use a reading or gloss hint, and must pass the masked-LM answerability check. Webb's 2009 finding that productive learning has broader effects is a real counter-argument for production-first. For a reading-oriented learner with 323 mostly receptive targets, it does not outweigh the doubled review load.

On scheduling, the FSRS documentation is unusually prescriptive. Desired retention is "the most important setting," the default is **90%**, and "above 90% the workload increases very quickly" ([Anki manual, deck options](https://github.com/ankitects/anki-manual/blob/main/src/deck-options.md)). The one habit FSRS cannot adapt to is pressing **Hard when you forgot**. Hard is a passing grade, and the manual explicitly sanctions using only Again and Good ([Anki manual, deck options](https://github.com/ankitects/anki-manual/blob/main/src/deck-options.md); [Anki manual, studying](https://github.com/ankitects/anki-manual/blob/main/src/studying.md)). Learning steps should be short and same-day, because "repeating a card multiple times in a single day does not significantly contribute to long-term memory." Parameter optimisation needs at least a few hundred reviews. The open benchmark of about **350M reviews** shows FSRS-6 and FSRS-7 well ahead of older models: log loss 0.346/0.340 versus HLR's 0.469 ([srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)). The often-quoted claim of 20–30% fewer reviews than SM-2 comes from simulation, not user trials ([ABC of FSRS](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/ABC-of-FSRS)). Tatsumoto's verdict that "FSRS appears to be a dead end" ([Setting up Anki](https://tatsumoto-ren.github.io/blog/setting-up-anki.html)) is a minority view against benchmark data and the communities' own adoption.

**Recommendation (d): card design.**

The **default card is a TSC**:

- The front shows the anchor sentence with the target **bolded**.
- The target has no furigana. Optional furigana covers only _non-target_ words above the learner's level.
- No audio plays on the front.
- No English appears on the front, since a gloss removes the retrieval act.
- For kana-only words and onomatopoeia, the sentence is essential context. For words with a kanji form, show the kanji form.

The **back** carries:

- the target's reading and pitch accent;
- the definition of the _sense used in this sentence_, monolingual once the learner is ready;
- the full sentence with furigana;
- a sentence translation, collapsed by default;
- word and sentence audio when licensable;
- the source and licence, with a "written for this deck" label where applicable;
- for extended senses, the core sense.

**Grading** uses **two buttons: Again/Good**, as swipe left/right plus visible buttons. Grade only the target's meaning and reading. Use TheMoeWay's rule: if the back made you understand the target better, it is Again ([TheMoeWay FAQ](https://learnjapanese.moe/faq/)).

**FSRS settings:**

- default parameters at **0.90 retention**, with per-user optimisation after several hundred reviews;
- learning steps of about 1m/10m and a 10m relearning step;
- a leech threshold of about **6 lapses**, between Tatsumoto's 4–6 and Anki's default 8 ([Anki leeches](https://github.com/ankitects/anki-manual/blob/main/src/leeches.md)).

When a card becomes a leech, prompt the learner to fix it in this order: swap in the next-ranked sentence, add a Lapis-style hint, and finally delete the card, in the AJATT spirit.

**Optional siblings** are enabled per word, only for the "use" tier, and released after the TSC matures. They are a constrained cloze/production card and, for kanji the learner wants to write, a Tatsumoto-style kana-to-kanji production TSC. Bury them relative to the recognition card.

Keep the **context-dependence escape hatch** available. Lapis and JPMN both offer a word-only or click-to-reveal variant, and Kaishi lets learners blur the sentence. If the learner reports recognising sentences instead of words, flip mature cards to word-front with the sentence on click.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the deck-building problem. It is not mainly a card-format debate, where the evidence and the communities largely agree. It is a **sentence-supply problem**. The format question resolves to "one bolded target, recognition first, Again/Good, FSRS at 0.90." The hard parts are three. Lemma-accurate retrieval must handle conjugation and polysemy. A context-dependence filter must stop corpus sentences from being worse than generated ones. And provenance discipline must govern the minority of words, probably concentrated in rare N1, literary and onomatopoeic items, for which no licensable native sentence exists. The single most valuable feature an app like this can have is capturing the **sentence the learner was reading when they looked the word up**, because it beats every ranked corpus candidate on relevance, sense accuracy and memorability. Ranked corpus sentences are the fallback, not the main source.

Real uncertainty remains. No study has tested sentences-per-word inside an SRS. Cousin-card interference under FSRS is unquantified. No retention study compares LLM-written and corpus sentences for Japanese. And the 323-word list's actual Tatoeba coverage still needs to be measured rather than extrapolated from a 16-word probe. The staged policy above (anchor first, diversify after stability, repair leeches by swapping sentences) has a useful side effect: the app collects the per-word lapse data needed to answer these questions for this learner.
