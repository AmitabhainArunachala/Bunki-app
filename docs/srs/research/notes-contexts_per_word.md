# Contexts per word: how many example sentences and how varied, for an advanced (N2–N1) Japanese SRS deck

Note on method: most publisher full texts (cambridge.org, nature.com, PMC, reading.ac.uk, hawaii.edu) were blocked by the network proxy, so most findings below come from abstracts and search-result summaries of the primary papers, not full-text reading. Effect sizes and designs are given only where an abstract or summary stated them. Nearly all studies use English L2 or pseudowords. None uses Japanese, and none studies SRS decks directly.

## 1. How many encounters are needed, and how do SRS repetitions relate?

### Takeaway

Repetition reliably helps, but no single number is "enough." Incidental-learning estimates run from about 6 to more than 20 encounters, and the meta-analytic effect of repetition is medium (r = .34). How informative the context is, and how much the learner has to retrieve, matter as much as the raw count. In an SRS deck, retrieval-based reviews are more efficient than incidental encounters, so the deck needs far fewer _distinct sentences_ than the "10+ encounters" figures suggest.

### Cited Findings

- **Meta-analysis (strong):** Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019) pooled 45 effect sizes from 26 studies (N = 1,918) and found a medium correlation between frequency and incidental learning, r = .34. Repetition effects varied with learner variables (age, prior vocabulary knowledge), with treatment variables (spaced learning, visual support, engagement, the range of encounter counts) and with method (nonwords, forewarning of a test, test format). — [Language Learning 69(3), 559–599](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12343)
- **Webb (2007):** 121 Japanese learners of English met target words 1, 3, 7 or 10 times. Ten tests measured orthography, paradigmatic and syntagmatic association, grammatical functions, syntax, and meaning/form. Knowledge grew as encounters increased, but 10 encounters still did not give full knowledge on every aspect. — [Applied Linguistics, ERIC EJ757328](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ757328); summary of design and results from [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/31064743_The_Effects_of_Repetition_on_Vocabulary_Knowledge)
- **Webb (2008):** Context quality may affect _meaning_ knowledge more than the number of encounters, while encounter count matters more for _form_. When an informative first context was followed by uninformative ones, meaning-recognition scores could be lower than after a single encounter ("backsliding"). — [Reading in a Foreign Language 20(2), ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ815123.pdf)
- **Range of thresholds:** Saragi, Nation & Meister (1978) proposed about 10 encounters. Horst et al. (1998), Waring & Takaki (2003) and Webb (2007) are cited for about 8, about 10 and about 12 respectively. Other studies cite 6 to more than 20. The spread is attributed partly to how guessable the word is from context. — summary at [EAP Foundation](https://www.eapfoundation.com/vocab/learn/incidental/); also [Webb 2008](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ815123.pdf)
- **Retrieval beats rich context for retention (strong; 3 experiments):** In van den Broek et al. (2018), recall of form and meaning, and recognition in a _new_ context, were better after retrieval practice than after inferring meaning from an informative context. Rich context helped comprehension but not retention. — [Language Learning 68, 546–585](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12285)
- **Spacing (strong as a general effect):** Spaced practice beats massed practice for L2 vocabulary. Expanding and equal spacing perform about the same, with at most a small expanding advantage in one study. Long lags cause more failures during learning but often give better long-term retention. — Nakata (2015), [SSLA 37, 677–711](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/EFFECTS-OF-EXPANDING-AND-EQUAL-SPACING-ON-SECOND-Nakata/2d47bcd6b29fd9a255d97cd94fea8bb860bf39b5); [J-STAGE ARELE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/arele/27/0/27_217/_pdf)
- **Spacing affects explicit, not tacit, knowledge (single study):** In contextual learning, spacing helped explicit vocabulary knowledge but not tacit knowledge. — Nakata & Elgort (2021), [Second Language Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0267658320927764)

### Inferences

- The "10–20 encounters" figures come from _incidental_ reading or listening, with no retrieval or feedback. An SRS review is an intentional, spaced, feedback-backed retrieval event, which is the strongest condition in the literature (van den Broek 2018; Nakata). One well-chosen sentence card reviewed 6–10 times over months probably gives more than 10 incidental encounters. However, it covers only one context, so it builds form–meaning mapping more than collocation, grammar and register knowledge (Webb 2007's multiple aspects).
- Webb (2008) suggests the _first_ context should be highly informative (a clear sentence plus a gloss). Later contexts can be less transparent, because the learner then has to retrieve the meaning, which helps retention. Weak first contexts risk "backsliding."
- Outside-deck reading and listening supply the extra encounters that build deeper knowledge. The deck does not have to provide all of them.

### Gaps

- No study found tests the number of _distinct sentences per word_ inside an SRS or FSRS system.
- No Japanese-specific encounter-threshold study was found (kanji compounds may differ because of morphological transparency).

## 2. Contextual variability: varied contexts vs the same context repeated

### Takeaway

The evidence is mixed but fairly consistent once stage of learning and outcome type are separated. Varied contexts help **generalisation, transfer to new contexts and delayed recognition**, and benefit learners with larger vocabularies. A **single consistent or anchored context** helps **early meaning learning** and recall of specific semantic features. The best-supported pattern is "anchor first, then diversify." A meta-analysis reports an overall moderate benefit of diversity (d = 0.44).

### Cited Findings

- **Meta-analysis (moderately strong; small k):** A systematic review of L1 and L2 studies found a significant effect of contextual diversity in behavioural studies, d = 0.44 (p < .001), based on 15 behavioural and 5 eye-tracking effect sizes. — [Applied Psycholinguistics, "The effect of contextual diversity on L1 and L2 word learning"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/effect-of-contextual-diversity-on-l1-and-l2-word-learning-a-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/56CB12725024BBE5DD57F374008F193D) (authors and year not visible; full text blocked)
- **Bolger, Balass, Landen & Perfetti (2008):** Varied semantic contexts improved meaning recall and supported an instance-based account: each encounter leaves a trace, and features shared across encounters become the abstracted meaning. — Discourse Processes 45(2), 122–159; summarised in [MDPI Educ. Sci. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci15091264)
- **Pagán & Nation (2019):** The same sentence ×4 was compared with 4 different sentences. Diverse-context words were learned more _slowly_ during training but did _better_ at the immediate posttest. Distributed practice slowed learning with no posttest benefit. Retrieval opportunities helped when contexts were diverse. — [Cognitive Science 43(1), e12705](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cogs.12705)
- **Johns, Dye & Jones (2016):** Diverse contexts strengthened word-form memory (faster, more accurate recognition), while uniform contexts gave better semantic-relatedness judgements, as their Semantic Distinctiveness Model predicts. — [Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 23(4), 1214–1220](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-015-0980-7)
- **Mak, Hsiao & Nation (2021):** Low diversity gave higher semantic-relatedness and old/new accuracy at the start of learning. High diversity paid off _after_ words had first been met in a coherent, familiar context ("anchoring"). — [Journal of Memory and Language 118, 104203](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/anchoring-and-contextual-variation-in-the-early-stages-of-inciden/)
- **Norman et al. (2023), large preregistered study (N = 239):** Each pseudoword appeared in 10 sentences, on 10 different topics or on 1 topic. Diverse training improved generalisation of meaning to _new_ contexts. Non-diverse training was better for applying the meaning in _familiar_ contexts. — [QJEP](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/17470218221126976)
- **Hulme, Begum, Nation & Rodd (2023), preregistered (N = 100):** Five paragraphs came from one narrative or five narratives. Diversity did not affect form learning, and more semantic features were recalled after a single narrative. Variability makes the earliest stage harder. — [Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 30(6), 2338–2350](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-023-02316-z)
- **Li et al. (2024):** Their title reports "null effects on learning word forms and opposing effects on learning word meanings" for diversity and anchoring (details not retrieved). — [QJEP](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/17470218231218990)
- **L1 vs FL contextual diversity:** Total encounters were held constant while words were spread across 1, 2, 4 or 8 texts, in the native and a foreign language. — [Scientific Reports 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-70922-1) (results not retrieved; full text blocked)
- **L2 moderator (repeated across summaries):** High contextual diversity beat low diversity on _delayed meaning recognition_. Learners with larger prior vocabularies benefited more, and varied input may hurt lower-proficiency learners. — [SSLA, "The effects of contextual diversity on incidental vocabulary learning"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-contextual-diversity-on-incidental-vocabulary-learning/F83A1EB55404E2E07FDC1695B0BC2BAE) (abstract-level only)

### Inferences

- Applied to N2–N1 learners, who have large vocabularies and are the group that benefits most from diversity:
  1. **Card 1** is a clear, informative "anchor" sentence showing the most typical use.
  2. **Cards 2–3** are added once card 1 is stable (for example, after graduating or reaching a stability of a few weeks). They come from clearly _different_ topics, registers or genres (news vs fiction vs conversation), to build transfer.
- Repeating the _same_ sentence across cards adds little. It speeds training but generalises worse (Pagán & Nation; Norman et al.).
- For recognition-only goals (reading comprehension), one anchor plus outside reading may be enough. Where active use or transfer matters, 2–3 varied contexts are better supported.

### Gaps

- The effect is mostly shown with pseudowords and short training sessions over days. No long-term (months) studies with real L2 words in SRS were found.
- The meta-analysis's moderators (L1 vs L2, test type) could not be retrieved.

## 3. Polysemous words: one example per sense?

### Takeaway

The best-known evidence (Verspoor & Lowie 2003) supports presenting a **core/prototypical sense** as a cue for learning figurative or extended senses. This argues for a core-sense example first, with sense-specific examples linked back to it, rather than unrelated sense lists. No research found tests "exactly one example per sense."

### Cited Findings

- **Verspoor & Lowie (2003):** In a series of experiments with Dutch learners of English, giving the core sense as a cue led to better guessing _and_ long-term retention of figurative senses than no cue or a non-literal cue. The proposed mechanism is "precise elaboration" linking the peripheral sense to the core. — [Language Learning 53(3), 547–586](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9922.00234); [author PDF](https://www.rug.nl/staff/w.m.lowie/verspoorlowie2003_-_language_learning.pdf)
- The instance-based view (Bolger et al. 2008) treats meaning as abstracted from features shared across encounters. Mixing distinct senses in "varied contexts" could therefore blur rather than sharpen each sense. This is an implication of the model, not a tested result. — [MDPI summary of Bolger et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci15091264)

### Inferences

- For a polysemous N2–N1 word, give one sentence per _target_ sense (only the senses the learner meets in their materials). Put the core-sense sentence first, and show the core meaning (or the kanji's literal meaning) on the back of extended-sense cards.
- Do not count different senses as "contextual diversity." Diversity within one sense helps transfer. Senses are separate form–meaning mappings and each needs its own anchor.
- For Japanese, kanji often encode the core image, which may serve as a natural core-sense cue (unverified for Japanese).

### Gaps

- No experiment found compares 1 vs 2+ examples per sense, or all senses at once vs staged.
- No Japanese-specific polysemy-teaching studies were found.

## 4. Collocations: learning words in chunks, and how many to show

### Takeaway

Collocations are learnable incidentally and gains grow with exposure, but the dose–response is unclear: 15 exposures in one study, a plateau at 4 in another. Distributed practice for verb–noun collocations has been studied directly. The evidence supports building example sentences around a word's highest-frequency collocations, but it does not set a number to show.

### Cited Findings

- **Webb, Newton & Chang (2013):** Collocations were met 1, 5, 10 or 15 times through reading-while-listening. Learning rose with exposure, and about 15 exposures were needed for sizeable learning. — Language Learning 63, 91–120; cited in [Pellicer-Sánchez 2017 summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286744272_Learning_L2_collocations_incidentally_from_reading)
- **Pellicer-Sánchez (2017):** Adjective + pseudoword collocations were met in a reading text. Collocational knowledge was retained one week later, with no significant difference between 4 and 8 occurrences. This contrasts with Webb et al. (2013). — [Language Teaching Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168815618428)
- Further studies on adjacency and prior vocabulary knowledge in collocation learning, and on distributed practice for verb–noun collocations, exist but were not read. — [ITL (Vilkaitė-Lozdienė)](https://benjamins.com/catalog/itl.17005.vil); [SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-distributed-practice-on-the-acquisition-of-verbnoun-collocations/AFDA91E8ECFDB03151056108E0341FA3)
- A retrieval-enhanced learning study (2026) examined contextual learning of L2 multi-word expressions. — [LTR, Tadayonifar & Entezari](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13621688261452314) (details not retrieved)

### Inferences

- In SRS, each sentence card is a natural carrier for one collocation. Choosing sentences 2–3 to show _different high-frequency collocates_ (for example, different verbs taking the noun, or noun + を + verb patterns) serves both contextual diversity and collocation learning.
- Because incidental collocation learning needs many exposures, showing the collocate in the cloze or highlighted target (for example, testing 「懸念を抱く」 rather than 「懸念」 alone) may be more efficient. This is an inference, not a directly tested result.

### Gaps

- No study found gives an optimal number of collocations per word to display.
- No study compares explicit collocation cards with word cards in SRS.

## 5. Rare/literary words vs high-frequency words: should the number of contexts differ?

### Takeaway

No direct study was found. Indirect evidence (difficulty and moderator effects, the role of prior knowledge, and the effectiveness of retrieval) suggests that rare and literary words merit **fewer** dedicated contexts (one anchor, recognition-focused). High-frequency or productive-use words merit **more** varied contexts. Opportunity cost also points this way: rare words will get few outside encounters, but they are needed mainly for recognition.

### Cited Findings

- Encounter thresholds vary with how guessable the word is from context, so harder-to-infer words need more encounters. — [Webb 2008](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ815123.pdf); [EAP Foundation](https://www.eapfoundation.com/vocab/learn/incidental/)
- Repetition effects are moderated by learners' vocabulary knowledge. — [Uchihara et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12343)
- Diversity mainly helps generalisation and transfer to new contexts. — [Norman et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/17470218221126976)

### Inferences

- High-frequency N2 words that the learner will meet constantly get more outside-deck encounters anyway. Extra deck sentences matter most for words the learner wants to _use_, and for mid-frequency words met too rarely outside the deck to build collocation and register knowledge.
- For rare or literary N1 words (for example, words found mainly in literary prose): use one strong anchor sentence, recognition-only, plus the kanji or core meaning. Add a second context only if the word keeps lapsing (a leech) or appears in the learner's reading.
- A practical allocation (heuristic, not evidence-validated):
  - Core or productive words: 2–3 sentences.
  - Mid-frequency recognition words: 1–2.
  - Rare or literary words: 1.

### Gaps

- No study was found that compares the optimal number of contexts across frequency bands. This is a real gap in the literature.

## 6. Spacing sibling cards (same word, different sentences): interference and burying

### Takeaway

Anki's sibling burying and the FSRS Helper's "Disperse Siblings" feature rest on a practical rationale: cards close together cue each other, which inflates success and reduces the effortful retrieval that drives retention. FSRS models each card independently, so it does not account for one card of a word reviewing the others. The research behind this is the general spacing and lag effect and retrieval-effort effects, not studies of siblings specifically.

### Cited Findings

- FSRS Helper's "Disperse Siblings" spreads the due dates of cards from the same note "to avoid interference & reminder." The add-on author says disabling it decreases efficiency. — [AnkiWeb FSRS Helper](https://ankiweb.net/shared/info/759844606)
- FSRS treats each card independently, even cards from the same note. — [Hacker News discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44791312) (informal source)
- Anki's sibling-burying option exists so related cards from one note are not shown on the same day. — [Anki forums](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/sibling-cards-being-buried-with-bury-siblings-turned-off/43770); [Wikipedia: Anki](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anki_(software)>)
- Separate notes (different sentences for the same word) are _not_ siblings in Anki. Tools such as "anki cousins" exist to bury related cards across notes. — [anki_cousins (GitHub)](https://github.com/AlexRiina/anki_cousins); [Anki forum request to link notes as siblings](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/manually-link-cards-from-different-notes-as-siblings/39455)
- Longer lags cause more failures during learning but often improve long-term retention. — [Nakata (2015)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/EFFECTS-OF-EXPANDING-AND-EQUAL-SPACING-ON-SECOND-Nakata/2d47bcd6b29fd9a255d97cd94fea8bb860bf39b5)
- In the reading study, diverse contexts plus retrieval opportunities helped learning. — [Pagán & Nation 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cogs.12705)

### Inferences

- If one word has several sentence cards on _different notes_, Anki will not bury them by default. A good-quality successful review of card A can "pre-review" card B the same day, so FSRS overestimates B's difficulty and stability. To avoid this:
  - Bury cousins (cards for the same word across notes), or
  - Stagger introduction: add sentence 2 only once sentence 1 has stability of about 2–3 weeks or more. This also matches the "anchor first, then diversify" evidence.
- Introducing all 3 sentences for a word on the same day as new cards is the worst case: massed exposure, cards cueing each other, and early diversity making the first stage harder (Hulme et al. 2023; Mak et al. 2021).
- An app that controls scheduling directly (like Bunki) can group cards by lemma, enforce a minimum gap between same-lemma reviews, and treat a review of any sentence for that lemma as partial evidence for the others.

### Gaps

- No peer-reviewed study was found on sibling or cousin interference in SRS specifically, or on the optimal minimum gap between them.
- No FSRS documentation was found quantifying the efficiency loss from undispersed siblings. The add-on author's claim is unquantified.
